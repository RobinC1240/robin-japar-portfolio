Enterprise case study

Turning UX operations data into an executive performance story

UX performance data existed across the enterprise, but leadership still lacked one clear view of how experience operations were performing against the year’s goals. I defined which data told that story, how it should be visualized for executive comprehension, and how automated analysis could turn reporting into actionable insight.

Recognized Digital Transformation Award For driving digital transformation through the automation of analytics data for machine learning and analytics reporting, replacing manual data entry. July 2019

The executive question

Leadership did not need more data. They needed one clear answer.

Every month produced more performance data across the UX ecosystem, but no single source showed whether the organization was actually delivering against the goals set for the year. The scorecard had to connect those signals into one coherent executive view.

1

question shaped the entire scorecard

Are UX operations achieving the goals set for the year, and where does leadership need to act? Every measure, visualization, and insight had to help answer that question.

What already existed

Journey analytics, voice of customer, channel performance, project status, and customer research, each useful in isolation but incomplete as an executive story.

What was missing

A single view that connected performance back to annual goals, explained what was driving the results, and showed where leadership attention was needed.

The opportunity was to turn reporting into interpretation.

How I built it

I started from the question, not the dashboard.

A scorecard assembled from whatever data happens to be available reports activity. To explain performance, the structure has to be decided before the visuals are, which meant starting with what leadership needed to decide and working backward to the measures.

First, I asked what leadership needed to decide.

I gathered requirements directly from stakeholders and executives rather than inferring them from existing reports. That established which questions the scorecard was accountable for answering, and which were somebody else's to answer.

This becamethe scope of what belonged on the page

Then, I organized data that lived in separate tools.

Analytics, research, channel, and project data were held by different organizations in different systems. I pulled them into one place and mapped each available measure against the requirement it could actually support.

What I decidedMeasures that answered none of the executive questions were cut, however easy they were to report.

This becameone dataset instead of a dozen reports

Finally, I specified how each measure should be seen.

Choosing the right number is only half the decision. How it is drawn determines whether a reader sees performance, or just a figure. I wrote a content strategy brief for the visualization of every piece of data in the scorecard, then worked directly with designers to build it.

What I decidedEvery measure had to show its target beside it, so a reader could see the gap without doing arithmetic.

This becamea scorecard that reads as an argument

The sequence of comprehension

The scorecard is ordered the way an executive reads.

1

Objective

What we said we would achieve this year.

2

Performance

Where the numbers actually landed against it.

3

Context

Channel behavior and project status that explain the movement.

4

Analysis

What the pattern means, written rather than left to inference.

5

Maturity

How far the experience is from where it needs to be.

6

Concerns

The friction and risk worth executive attention.

7

Action

The improvements to prioritize next.

A leader who stops reading after the first screen should still know whether the year is on track.

The artifact

One page, read top to bottom, that answers the year.

The scorecard runs as a single scrolling page rather than a set of tabs, so the argument holds its order. A reader cannot reach the recommendations without passing the performance that justifies them.

Performance against intent

Every KPI carries its target beside the current value, so the gap is visible without calculation. The trend sits underneath, because direction changes what a gap means.

Comparison across journeys

Journeys share one index and one scale, which makes them comparable at a glance and makes an outlier obvious.

Interpretation, written

Where a pattern needs explaining, the scorecard says so in words. Analysis is not left for the reader to infer from a chart.

Attention, directed

The page ends on friction and recommended strategies, so the last thing a reader sees is what to do about it.

Performance against target

A number on its own does not tell a leader anything.

Each journey KPI shows the current score, the target beneath it, the month-over-month change, and six months of trend. Four pieces of information in one component, because any one of them alone invites the wrong conclusion.

A score of 56 percent means nothing until you know the target was 53 percent and the line has been climbing for two months.

Comparison

Five journeys, one scale, and a reason attached to every movement.

The index sits at the center with the annual goal beside it. Each journey radiates out with its own component measures, so a reader can see both the whole and the part without switching views.

Every movement carries a one-line explanation. That was deliberate: a percentage change invites a question, and the scorecard should answer it in place rather than generate a follow-up meeting.

From analysis to action

The page ends where the decisions start.

The maturity scale places the current experience against where it needs to be across five pillars, which turns an abstract quality conversation into a position on a chart.

Underneath it, the friction points and improvement strategies are stated plainly. By the time a reader reaches them, the evidence for each one has already been laid out above.

Making it run

A monthly report that depends on manual data entry does not survive.

The scorecard had to be produced every month, from data owned by several different organizations. Assembled by hand, it would have been abandoned within a quarter, or worse, published late and wrong.

I worked to automate the analytics data behind it, removing the manual entry step and feeding machine learning and analytics reporting from the same pipeline. That is what the Digital Transformation Award recognized, and it is the reason the scorecard became a standing part of performance governance rather than a one-off deck.

What it changed

Reporting became governance.

The scorecard gave leadership a single place to see whether the year was on track, and a defensible basis for prioritizing what came next. The same instinct, deciding what evidence belongs in front of a decision-maker, runs through the taxonomy and journey work as well.
